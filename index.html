Course Description

This course explores spatial narratives in the unbuilt world. From games, movies, AR/VR, to metaverse, students will explore the idea of space in the digital realm. The discussion will be on: How does one interact with space? And how does that interaction with space shape a spatial narrative?

This workshop encompasses both theoretical readings on spatial narratives as well as learning and working with three-dimensional software in order to compose a holistic perspective of three-dimensional narratives in the virtual realm. Students will be required to play virtual games, study their spaces and narratives, and create digital narratives.

This semester the analysis of games is expanded. A level designer walks you through a building without saying a word, using light, threshold, compression, and sightline to do it. That is architecture doing narrative work, and it can be documented. Students will take their assigned game apart — its design architecture first, then its mechanics — and draw it the way architects already know how to draw a building. Software runs alongside from the first week; the analysis is not a gate you pass before the fun starts.

Each student is then assigned a medium — a section, a plan, an axonometric — and the story has to be told through it. Not as a drawing exercise. The medium becomes your camera in Unreal Engine, and it does not move.

Learning Goals

  1. Cultivating an understanding of the history and theory of virtual design
  2. Learning software skills in mesh-modeling and SubD in Rhino 8. Students will also learn to implement, animate, and film in Unreal Engine 5
  3. Developing spatial narratives and experimenting with novel ways to explore user interfaces through gamification
  4. Reading a game space as built work: documenting its design architecture, mechanics, and pacing through diagrams

Course Expectations

No text. No dialogue. No interface.

Strip all three and see what is left. If the story does not survive, the architecture was never carrying it — the words were. Everything you need is already in the light, the threshold, and the sequence.

The camera obeys your medium.

All semester. Assigned axonometric means an orthographic camera, locked. Assigned section means you cut the model and sit in the cut. No sweeping hero shot is coming to rescue a weak idea, which is exactly the point.

Something every week.

Upload progress to the class drive every week, however ugly or unfinished. This is how studios work and it is how you avoid discovering a broken build the night before a review. Discord is for everything in between.

Media (Assigned)

Architecture has always told stories through drawing conventions, and games have been quietly stealing them for years — Monument Valley is an axonometric, Inside is a section, Gorogoa is a panel grid. Each student is assigned one. It governs the drawings you produce and the camera you set up in Unreal Engine, and it does not move. That is the point: a locked orthographic view cannot be rescued by a swooping camera, so the space itself has to do the storytelling.

Section: the model is cut and the camera sits in the cut plane. Inside, Limbo

Plan: top-down, where narrative is carried by choreography of movement. Death's Door

Axonometric: orthographic and locked, with no vanishing point. Monument Valley, Hades

Elevation: a flat, fixed camera with narrative built in parallax layers. GRIS

Worm's-Eye / Reflected Ceiling Plan: the camera only looks up.

Exploded Assembly: the building comes apart as the narrative advances.

Collage / Photomontage: narrative assembled in layered plates. Kentucky Route Zero

Panel Grid: several simultaneous cameras read together. Gorogoa

One-Point Perspective: a fixed vanishing point and a symmetrical frame.

Cutaway Diorama: walls removed, the interior seen whole.

Assignment: Game Space Analysis

Weeks 1 through 3, running in parallel with the Rhino and Unreal workshops. Play your group's game properly, then take it apart in four layers — design architecture first, mechanics second — and present it in Week 3. Nobody is asking whether you liked it. The question is how it was built, and whether you can prove it with drawings. This presentation is the Demo portion of your grade.

1. Design Architecture: how is the world structured?

The critical path against the optional space. Gates and the keys that open them. Hub, open field, or corridor. Where the path forks, and where the designer quietly makes sure it returns.

2. Spatial Grammar: how does the space instruct without instructions?

Landmarks, sightlines, thresholds, compression and release. Turn off the waypoint marker and ask what is left telling you where to go. Every game builds a private language of color and material meaning climbable, dangerous, look here. Find it and write it down.

3. Game Mechanics: what can the player do, and what does it mean?

The verbs the player is given, and the loop they sit inside. Then the question that actually matters: how does the mechanic carry the theme? In What Remains of Edith Finch the mechanic changes with every story because every story is a different mind. In Stray you are held low to the ground because the world was not built for you. That is not decoration. That is the argument.

4. Pacing: how does the experience rise and fall?

Intensity across time — conflict, traversal, quiet, cutscene. Pay attention to the silences. They are placed as deliberately as the set pieces, and they are usually doing more work.

Required Diagrams

Six drawings, produced by hand or digitally. Screenshots may support them but do not replace them.

  1. Level graph. Nodes and edges, with gates marked and the critical path distinguished.
  2. Plan of one level, redrawn and annotated with landmarks and sightlines.
  3. Section through a significant vertical moment, showing what becomes visible and when.
  4. Sightline diagram of a single reveal, drawn as cones of vision.
  5. Core loop diagram. Action, feedback, reward, and the next action.
  6. Intensity curve across thirty minutes of play.

In Week 8 you will draw these same six diagrams for your own project. The analysis is not a separate exercise; it is the method you will design with.
