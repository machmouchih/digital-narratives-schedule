Course Information

Course Description

This course explores spatial narratives in the unbuilt world. From games, movies, AR/VR, to metaverse, students will explore the idea of space in the digital realm. The discussion will be on: How does one interact with space? And how does that interaction with space shape a spatial narrative?

This workshop encompasses both theoretical readings on spatial narratives as well as learning and working with three-dimensional software in order to compose a holistic perspective of three-dimensional narratives in the virtual realm. Students will be required to play virtual games, study their spaces and narratives, and create digital narratives.

This semester the game analysis is expanded. Students take their assigned game apart in four layers, starting with its design architecture and moving to its mechanics, and document it in six diagrams. Rhino and Unreal begin in Week 1 and run alongside the analysis. Each student is also assigned a medium, which governs both the drawings and the camera setup in Unreal for the rest of the semester. The second half of the semester is spent designing and building an original game mechanic.

The primary reference is An Architectural Approach to Level Design by Christopher Totten, supported by readings and tutorials provided throughout the semester.

Learning Goals

  1. Cultivating an understanding of the history and theory of virtual design.
  2. Learning mesh-modeling and SubD in Rhino 8, and implementing, animating, and filming in Unreal Engine 5.
  3. Developing spatial narratives and exploring user interfaces through gamification.
  4. Reading a game space as built work through diagrams.
  5. Designing and building an original game mechanic.
  6. Incorporating A.I. tools into the Rhino and Unreal workflow.

03

Final Project

Each student creates their own digital narrative and three-dimensional game space in Unreal Engine 5, developed across four phases. The first two phases run in the first half of the semester and conclude at the midterm. The last two run in the second half and conclude at the final review.

1

Game Space Analysis

Running alongside the Rhino and Unreal workshops in Weeks 1 to 3. Play your group's game, take it apart in four layers, and present the analysis in Week 3. You are documenting how the game is built rather than reviewing it, and the analysis is presented in drawings. This presentation is the Demo portion of your grade.

The four layers ▾

Design Architecture: how is the world structured?

Critical path against optional space. Gates and the keys that open them. Hub, open field, or corridor. Where the path forks and where it returns.

Spatial Grammar: how does the space instruct without instructions?

Landmarks, sightlines, thresholds, compression and release. Turn the waypoint marker off and note what still guides you through the space. Every game builds a color and material language for climbable, dangerous, and worth your attention.

Game Mechanics: what can the player do, and what does it mean?

The verbs the player is given and the core loop they sit inside, and how the mechanic carries the theme. In What Remains of Edith Finch the mechanic changes with each story, because each story belongs to a different character. In Stray the player moves at cat height, which changes how the city reads.

Pacing: how does the experience rise and fall?

Intensity across time: conflict, traversal, quiet, cutscene. Note where the silences are placed and what they do.

Six Required Diagrams

  1. Level graph. Nodes and edges, with gates marked and the critical path distinguished.
  2. Plan of one level, redrawn and annotated with landmarks and sightlines.
  3. Section through a significant vertical moment, showing what becomes visible and when.
  4. Sightline diagram of a single reveal, drawn as cones of vision.
  5. Core loop diagram. Action, feedback, reward, and the next action.
  6. Intensity curve across thirty minutes of play.
2

Verb, Part, and Medium

You will be assigned one Architectural Verb and one Medium. Your task is to interpret this verb by choosing one Building Part as your primary tool for storytelling, and to tell that story through your assigned medium. The result is presented at the midterm as an eight-second loop in Unreal Engine.

Building Part · Choose One

Your primary tool for storytelling. You work with this one part for the whole semester, and the rest of the scene supports it.

  • Wall / Façade · separation and enclosure, and the public face of a building.
  • Floor / Ground Plane · what the body moves across. Level change and material underfoot.
  • Ceiling / Roof · the overhead condition. Shelter, weight, and openings to the sky.
  • Door / Opening · controlled passage between one space and another.
  • Window / Aperture · the framed view, and what it shows or withholds.
  • Stair / Ramp · movement through height, and the rhythm of that movement.
  • Column / Structure · the order and rhythm of the space, and how the building stands.
  • Pathway / Corridor · the route, its length, and the pace it sets.
Architectural Verbs, assigned ▾
  • Framing a View
  • Guiding Movement
  • Creating Thresholds
  • Compressing and Releasing
  • Ascending and Descending
  • Sheltering and Exposing
  • Revealing and Concealing
  • Scaling and Dominating
  • Grounding and Floating
  • Reflecting and Distorting
Media, assigned ▾

Architecture tells stories through drawing conventions, and games use the same ones. Monument Valley is an axonometric. Inside is a section. Gorogoa is a panel grid. Your assigned convention sets the drawings you produce and the camera you build in Unreal Engine. A locked view cannot be fixed with camera movement, so the narrative has to be carried by the space itself.

  • Section · the model is cut and the camera sits in the cut plane. Inside, Limbo
  • Plan · top down, narrative carried by choreography of movement. Death's Door
  • Axonometric · orthographic and locked, with no vanishing point. Monument Valley, Hades
  • Elevation · a flat, fixed camera with narrative built in parallax layers. GRIS
  • Worm's-Eye or Reflected Ceiling Plan · the camera only looks up.
  • Exploded Assembly · the building comes apart as the narrative advances.
  • Cutaway Diorama · walls removed, the interior seen whole.
  • One-Point Perspective · a fixed vanishing point and a symmetrical frame.
  • Collage or Photomontage · narrative assembled in layered plates. Kentucky Route Zero
  • Panel Grid · several simultaneous cameras read together. Gorogoa
  • Text and Typography · language placed in space as inscription, signage, or projection. Letterforms are built, sited, and lit like any other element. The Stanley Parable, NaissanceE
3

Your Game Mechanic

New this semester

In the first half of the semester you documented how other people's mechanics work. In the second half you build one. Every student designs a single core mechanic, specifies it, implements it in Blueprints, and tests it on a classmate.

The specification is written in Week 8, before any Blueprint work begins. Work takes place in Blueprints inside Unreal Engine 5, which requires no additional software and operates directly on the scene students are already developing. Prior programming experience is not required.

Mechanic Specification · One Page

  • The verb. What can the player do that they could not do before?
  • Input. How is it triggered, and how does the player learn that without being told?
  • Feedback. What changes in the world so the player knows it worked?
  • Limits. What stops it, and what does the limit mean?
  • Meaning. What does this mechanic say that a cutscene could not?

Requirement

Build one mechanic and get it working rather than several that are incomplete. In Week 11 a classmate will play your scene without instructions. If they do not find the mechanic, it needs more work.

4

The Interactive World

The mechanic is placed in a world built out in Unreal Engine 5, using dynamic materials, advanced lighting, atmosphere, and sound. Students document their process and present the final project in a clear and compelling way, filmed through their assigned medium's camera and packaged for the final review.

Project Criteria

  • Lighting: how does the quality of light shape the spatial experience?
  • Materiality: how do different materials shape the digital narrative?
  • Interaction with space and movement: how does one interact with the space?
  • Narrative: what is the essential narrative that this experience delivers?

Midterm debrief. Discussing player agency and interactive storytelling. Turning the six diagrams onto your own project. Course Expectations

Students are expected to be actively engaged throughout the course. Because the course meets online and only once per week, the following expectations govern the work carried out between sessions. Each contributes to the participation portion of the final grade.

Expectation 01

Play is Research

Students are required to play their assigned games and study their spaces and narratives. Take notes and capture screenshots while you play. This material feeds directly into your own project.

Expectation 02

The Camera Follows the Medium

Your assigned medium sets your camera for the semester. Axonometric means orthographic and locked. Section means the model is cut and the camera sits in the cut.

Expectation 03

Weekly Progress

Upload progress work to the class drive every week, finished or not. Discord is used for informal communication and technical support between sessions.

06

Use of Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence tools are part of the pipeline in this course, taught in Weeks 2, 4, 9, and 10. Their use is treated as a normal part of the working method rather than as an exception to it, and students are not required to avoid them.

They are used to produce assets and to assist with scripting. The diagrams, the mechanic specification, and the narrative are the student's own work, and students should be able to account for every generated asset in their scene.

Week 2 covers previsualization, using image and video generation for moodboards, concept frames, and testing an idea before it is modeled. Week 4 covers asset generation, including text and image to 3D for props and secondary geometry; generated meshes come in dirty and are cleaned in Rhino, and your Building Part is modeled by you. Week 9 covers drafting, reading, and debugging Blueprint logic with an assistant, which is useful for structure but unreliable on node-level specifics. Week 10 covers texture and material generation, video-based motion capture, and generated ambience.

Work submitted in this course shall be the original creation of its author, and is allowed to contain the work of others so long as there is appropriate attribution. Document your use of A.I. tools: what you generated, what you kept, and what you had to fix.

The Requirement

Students should be able to explain what they generated, what they kept, and what they changed.

Evidence

  • Documentation of A.I. use at both reviews
  • Attribution for generated assets
  • Verbal explanation on request during a review

Attendance

Students are expected to be present during class. Absences accruing more than three times during the semester will result in a reduction of grades at the discretion of the instructor. Lateness of more than 15 minutes will be counted as an absence unless communicated in advance. If there are any extenuating circumstances, please reach out to the instructor.

Deadlines

Students should complete assignments to the best of their ability and submit them on time. If circumstances require a late submission, contact the instructor before the assignment is due. In the event of an emergency, contact both the instructor and your student advisor as soon as possible.

Software and Assistance

Rhino 8 and Unreal Engine 5. Install both during Week 1 and report any problems on Discord early rather than the night before a review. Tutorials will be provided throughout the course in addition to technical support on Discord.
